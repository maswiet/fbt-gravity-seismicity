\begin{keypoints}
\item We map the Moho of the whole Indonesian archipelago from satellite gravity:
the first continuous crustal-thickness model of the region
\item The model fits 105 receiver-function depths to 5.8~km and matches the
independent seismic AusMoho at the Australian margin ($r=0.86$)
\item It equals CRUST1.0 at the seismic points, while the GOCE-only GEMMA model is
$\sim$7~km too shallow: global models are inadequate here
\end{keypoints}

\begin{abstract}
Mapping the crust--mantle boundary (Moho) is a first-order requirement for studying
an active region's crust, yet across the Indonesian archipelago it is known only
from sparse seismic stations. We estimate it from satellite gravity, which senses
mass uniformly onshore and offshore, using the fast spherical inversion of Uieda
and Barbosa (2017): the anomalous Moho is discretised into tesseroids and inverted
with Bott's method under Tikhonov regularisation. We apply this to the whole
archipelago, calibrating three hyperparameters against 105 receiver-function Moho
depths. The preferred model fits the seismic Moho to $+1.2\pm5.8$~km and agrees
with the independent AusMoho model along the Australian margin (bias $-0.1$~km,
$r=0.86$). At the seismic stations it equals CRUST1.0, whereas the GOCE-only GEMMA
model is 7.4~km too shallow. The Moho thickens beneath the arcs and collision belts
and thins beneath the marginal basins, tracking the region's tectonics.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Plain Language Summary}
The Moho is the boundary between Earth's rigid crust and the mantle beneath it; how
deep it lies tells us where the crust is thick or thin, a basic ingredient for
studying earthquakes and volcanoes. Across Indonesia, one of the most tectonically
active regions on Earth, this depth is measured only near scattered seismic
stations, so most of the archipelago and its seas remain blank on the map. The pull
of gravity, measured uniformly from satellites, offers a way to fill these gaps:
thicker or lighter crust weighs differently, and from that signal the depth of the
Moho can be reconstructed everywhere at once. We do so for the whole of Indonesia
and check the result against 105 independent seismic measurements. Our map agrees
with the seismic values as closely as the widely used global model CRUST1.0, even
though it is five times more detailed, and it corrects the global gravity model
GEMMA, which is about seven kilometres too shallow here. The result is a
continuous, openly available Moho map for the region.

\section{Introduction}
The Mohorovi\v{c}i\'{c} discontinuity (Moho) marks the base of the crust, where
the P-wave velocity increases from $\sim$6.5--7 to $\sim$8~km\,s$^{-1}$ and the
density from $\sim$2.8--2.9 to $\sim$3.3~Mg\,m$^{-3}$. Its depth --- the crustal
thickness --- is a first-order constraint on the isostatic and flexural state of
the lithosphere and on the tectonic processes that shaped it \cite{watts2001}. It
is also of practical necessity: the crustal velocity model that hinges on Moho
depth underlies earthquake location, magnitude estimation and seismic-hazard
assessment. A continuous, accurate Moho is thus a foundational dataset --- and
across Indonesia it is largely missing.

Indonesia is among the most tectonically complex regions on Earth, at the junction
of the Indo-Australian, Sunda (Eurasian), Philippine Sea and Pacific plates
\cite{hamilton1979,hall2002}. Oceanic Indo-Australian lithosphere subducts along
the Sunda trench beneath Sumatra and Java; farther east the Australian continental
margin collides with the Banda arc, whose sharply curved geometry reflects slab
rollback \cite{spakman2010}; Sulawesi and Papua record collision and ophiolite
emplacement; and back-arc and marginal basins --- the Banda, Celebes and Java seas
and the Makassar Strait --- floor much of the region. Across these settings the
Moho ranges from $\sim$10~km beneath the oceanic basins to more than 40~km beneath
the arcs and collision belts, often within a few tens of kilometres. Seismic
methods --- receiver-function $Ps$ conversions and $H$--$\kappa$ stacking
\cite{zhu2000}, active-source refraction and ambient-noise tomography --- give the
most direct depths, but they sense the crust only near stations, which in Indonesia
cluster on land and along the volcanic arc \cite{zhang2023}. As a result, eastern
Indonesia and essentially all of the intervening seas remain uncharted.

Where local data are absent, two global models are commonly adopted. CRUST1.0
\cite{laske2013} is a $1^{\circ}$ compilation that, over Indonesia, is interpolated
from sparse controls; GEMMA \cite{reguzzoni2013,reguzzoni2015,rossi2022} is a
$0.5^{\circ}$ Moho inverted globally from GOCE gravity gradients under a simplified
two-layer density parameterisation. Neither is tuned to Indonesian data, and both
are too coarse to resolve the sharp arc and fore-arc contrasts of the archipelago
--- indeed, we find GEMMA to be systematically $\sim$7~km too shallow at Indonesian
seismic stations (Section~4). A dedicated, regionally calibrated model is therefore
needed.

Gravity supplies what seismology lacks here: a continuous, homogeneous field,
sensitive to the crust--mantle density contrast and available uniformly onshore and
offshore from satellite missions such as GOCE and GRACE \cite{kvas2021}. Recovering
an interface such as the Moho from gravity is, however, nonlinear and non-unique ---
a given anomaly can be reproduced by trading density contrast against depth --- so
the inversion must be regularised and anchored to independent data. \citeA{uieda2017}
solved the forward and inverse problem efficiently in \emph{spherical} coordinates,
combining Bott's \cite{bott1960} iteration, tesseroid (spherical-prism) forward
modelling \cite{uieda2016} and first-order Tikhonov regularisation, and applied it
to the South American Moho. In this work, we transfer that method, unchanged in
principle, to the whole of Indonesia; we calibrate its three hyperparameters against
an independent compilation of 105 receiver-function Moho depths, and we validate the
result against the seismic AusMoho model \cite{kennett2011} along the Australian
margin and against CRUST1.0 and GEMMA at the seismic stations. The outcome is the
first continuous, public-data gravity Moho for the archipelago.

\section{Data and Methods}
We use the satellite-only gravity model GOCO06s \cite{kvas2021}, synthesised to
spherical-harmonic degree/order 300 on a regular grid at 4~km constant height, and,
as a higher-resolution variant, the altimetry free-air anomaly \cite{sandwell2014};
Earth relief is from SRTM15+ \cite{tozer2019}. Validation rests on our compilation of 105
receiver-function (RF) Moho depths across Indonesia \cite{fauzi2021}
($96.3^{\circ}$--$140.7^{\circ}$E, $10.2^{\circ}$S--$5.2^{\circ}$N; 20--40~km deep,
mean 30.4~km; 31 on Sumatra, 24 on Java, 50 in eastern Indonesia, none offshore;
Table~S1, Figure~\ref{fig:compare}a), obtained by $H$--$\kappa$ stacking of teleseismic
$Ps$ conversions. Being derived from seismic velocities, these depths are independent
of the gravity field inverted here. The most comprehensive comparable compilation is
\citeA{zhang2023}.

The gravity disturbance is the observed field minus the closed-form WGS84 normal
gravity,
\begin{equation}
\delta(P) = g(P) - \gamma(P),
\label{eq:disturbance}
\end{equation}
so that it retains only the effect of masses anomalous with respect to the
reference ellipsoid. Every mass effect is forward modelled with tesseroids
(spherical prisms) that honour Earth curvature \cite{uieda2016}: a tesseroid of
density $\rho$ produces at the computation point $P=(r_P,\phi_P,\lambda_P)$ the
radial attraction
\begin{equation}
g_z(P) = G\rho \int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}\!\!\int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2}\!\!
\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{(r_P-r\cos\psi)\,r^{2}\cos\phi}{\ell^{3}}
\;dr\,d\phi\,d\lambda,
\label{eq:tesseroid}
\end{equation}
with $\ell=(r_P^{2}+r^{2}-2r_Pr\cos\psi)^{1/2}$ and
$\cos\psi=\sin\phi_P\sin\phi+\cos\phi_P\cos\phi\cos(\lambda-\lambda_P)$, evaluated
by Gauss--Legendre quadrature \cite{uieda2016}. Removing the topographic and
bathymetric effect (land density $2670$, ocean contrast $-1640$~kg\,m$^{-3}$)
leaves the Bouguer disturbance, our data vector $\mathbf{d}^{o}$. The anomalous Moho
is one tesseroid per grid cell between the reference depth $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and
the true depth $z_k$ (the unknowns $\mathbf{p}$), with contrast $-\Delta\rho$ where
the Moho is deeper than $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $+\Delta\rho$ where shallower
\cite{uieda2017}.

We estimate $\mathbf{p}$ by minimising the regularised goal function
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(\mathbf{p}) = \big\|\mathbf{d}^{o}-\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{p})\big\|^{2}
 + \mu\,\big\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{p}\big\|^{2},
\label{eq:goal}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}$ is the first-difference operator and $\mu$ the regularisation
weight. Following \citeA{bott1960} and \citeA{silva2014}, the dense Jacobian is
replaced by the diagonal Bouguer-plate form
$\mathbf{A}=-2\pi G\,\Delta\rho\,\mathbf{I}$ (the minus sign expressing that a
deeper Moho lowers gravity), so each Gauss--Newton step solves a sparse, constant,
symmetric-positive-definite system. Depths are clipped to 3--70~km and iterated to
convergence; for $\mu=0$ this reduces to the classical Bott update,
\begin{equation}
\Delta z_j = \frac{d^{o}_j - d_j(\mathbf{p})}{2\pi G\,\Delta\rho}.
\label{eq:bott}
\end{equation}
The three hyperparameters are set in two steps \cite{uieda2017}: $\mu$ by hold-out
cross-validation, then $(z_{\mathrm{ref}},\Delta\rho)$ against the seismic Moho
(Figure~S2).

\textbf{Absolute level and the tie to AusMoho.} The inversion determines the Moho
\emph{relief} from the Bouguer disturbance; its \emph{absolute} level is carried by
the two global scalars $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\Delta\rho$. Through the reference
model and the plate relation~(\ref{eq:bott}), a deeper $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ or a
smaller $\Delta\rho$ shifts and rescales the entire surface without changing its
shape, so fixing this pair against seismic depths is what pins the model to an
absolute datum. The 105 RF depths \cite{fauzi2021} provide that control over most of
the archipelago, but they thin toward the southeast, at the Indonesia--Australia
border, where the absolute level would otherwise rest on extrapolation. We therefore
extend the inversion domain south to $15^{\circ}$S so that it overlaps the seismic
AusMoho grid \cite{kennett2011}, and use AusMoho as an independent absolute-depth
reference along that margin. Because $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\Delta\rho$ are single
numbers applied over the whole domain, a level calibrated on the inland RF depths
must also hold at the margin: the near-zero bias obtained against AusMoho over the
adjacent northern Australian mainland (Section~4), which contains no Indonesian
stations, shows that it does --- tying the southeastern Moho to the Australian
seismic datum and joining the two surfaces continuously across the border. AusMoho
thus controls the absolute level; it is not an input to the gravity inversion. Full
derivations, diagnostics and a sediment test are in Text~S1 and Figures~S1--S2.

\section{Results}
Cross-validation selects $\mu=10^{-10}$, i.e.\ an essentially unregularised
inversion. The seismic misfit decreases monotonically with $\Delta\rho$ --- a
density--depth trade-off whose raw minimum runs to unphysical contrasts --- but the
fit is only weakly sensitive over the physical range: at $z_{\mathrm{ref}}=35$~km
the RMS is 6.2, 6.0, 5.9 and 5.8~km at $\Delta\rho=400,450,500,600$~kg\,m$^{-3}$. We
therefore adopt a physical $\Delta\rho=500$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ with
$z_{\mathrm{ref}}=35$~km, which degrades the fit by only 0.3~km relative to 400 yet
is independently supported by AusMoho (below). $\Delta\rho$ is thus best read as an
\emph{effective} contrast. Bott's iteration converged in 15--20 steps.

The preferred model (Figure~\ref{fig:moho}) yields Moho depths of $\sim$7--59~km:
thick (35--45~km) beneath the Sunda, Sulawesi and Banda arcs and the Papuan
highlands, and thin (10--20~km) beneath the marginal and oceanic basins, with the
35-km contour outlining the arc and continental blocks. It differs from the 105
seismic depths by $+1.2\pm5.8$~km (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}), as good as the
identical method in South America (1.2, 6.8~km); the median is $+1.2$~km, the
interquartile range 6.9~km, and 63\% and 89\% of stations fall within $\pm5$ and
$\pm10$~km. This scatter is, in fact, at the floor set by the data: CRUST1.0 --- a
seismically referenced compilation --- does no better at these same stations
(5.3~km), and gravity Moho models routinely differ from seismic and from one
another by 4--8~km \cite{vandermeijde2013,reguzzoni2015}. The point correlation is
modest ($r=0.28$) for two reasons. First, the RF depths span only a narrow
20--40~km range and are clustered, which compresses the correlation. Second, and
more physically, the smooth $\sim$67-km gravity field imposes an amplitude
compression: an ordinary-least-squares fit of estimated on seismic depth has a
slope of only 0.20 (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}), so the thinnest crust is
overestimated and the thickest underestimated, while the mean trend is still
tracked (binned-mean $r=0.94$). Tellingly, CRUST1.0 shows the \emph{same}
compression at these points (slope 0.23), so the low slope reflects the reference
data and the point-versus-footprint scale mismatch, not our inversion. The
grid-to-grid AusMoho comparison, which matches like with like, is the fairer test
and yields $r=0.86$. Refining the grid to $0.25^{\circ}$ with GOCO06s does not
improve the fit, because at degree/order 300 the shortest resolved half-wavelength
is $\approx$67~km; the altimetry field recovers finer detail and near-zero bias
(Table~S2).

\textbf{Tie to AusMoho.} We anchor the southeastern part of the model, where
Indonesia borders Australia, to the seismic AusMoho compilation \cite{kennett2011}.
This link is two-way: our southern and western Indonesian RF depths \cite{fauzi2021}
were in turn contributed to the updated AusMoho surface (B.~L.~N.~Kennett, personal
communication, 2022), so along the shared margin the two datasets are consistent by
construction, which is precisely what makes AusMoho a suitable tie there. A
genuinely independent check is available farther south: over the northern Australian
mainland ($15^{\circ}$--$11.5^{\circ}$S, $N=369$), constrained by Australian seismic
data alone and untouched by our inversion, our gravity Moho matches AusMoho with a
mean difference of $-0.1$~km, a standard deviation of 4.8~km and $r=0.86$ --- strong
evidence that the method recovers \emph{absolute} depth, not merely relative
variations. Crust thickens to 39--50~km at the Timor--Banda collision front.

\textbf{Global models.} Sampled at the 105 stations (Table~\ref{tab:compare};
Figures~\ref{fig:scatter}--\ref{fig:compare}), our model equals CRUST1.0 (std 5.8
vs 5.3~km) despite being independent and five times finer --- and although CRUST1.0
itself incorporates some of the same RF results as priors. GEMMA, by contrast, is
systematically 7.4~km too shallow (RMS 10.2~km), with the largest deficits
(15--24~km) at the fore-arc and island-arc transitions of southern Java and the
Banda arc, where its coarse grid and global density model cannot capture the
crustal thickening that both the seismic stations and our inversion resolve.
Spatially (Figure~\ref{fig:compare}), our model agrees with CRUST1.0 to within
$\pm5$~km in the continental interior and diverges (deeper) mainly along the
Sunda--Java trench; it is deeper than GEMMA almost everywhere. Grid-to-grid
(Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}d--f) our model matches CRUST1.0 in amplitude (slope 0.78)
and reproduces GEMMA's spatial pattern (slope 0.93) but at a $\sim$8~km deeper,
seismically more consistent level.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
Even from a satellite field limited to $\sim$67-km wavelengths, we recover a Moho
that reproduces the first-order crustal architecture of Indonesia: thick crust
follows the volcanic arc and the Sulawesi--Banda--Papua collision belts, thin crust
marks the oceanic marginal basins, and the Holocene volcanoes sit along the
thick-crust arc (Figures~S3--S4). The station benchmark shows the model to be
competitive with the seismically compiled CRUST1.0 and markedly better than the
purely gravimetric GEMMA, quantifying why global models are inadequate for
Indonesia and motivating a regional treatment.

The limitations follow directly from the method and data. Validation is land-biased
and cannot constrain the offshore Moho directly; resolution is set by the satellite
gravity, not the grid; and a single, uniform $\Delta\rho$ over oceanic-to-continental
crust is a simplification. Because the diagonal Jacobian assigns all anomalous
gravity to the Moho, the subducting Sunda--Banda slabs are not separated and can
deepen the estimate near the trenches --- consistent with the deeper-than-CRUST1.0
signature there and with the largest residuals at the subduction fronts. Rather than
a formal uncertainty map, we give a sensitivity bound: across
$\Delta\rho=400$--$600$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ the seismic RMS moves by $<0.5$~km, and the
5.8~km scatter is an empirical error at the stations. A CRUST1.0 sediment correction
did not improve the fit (Text~S1), and dedicated offshore refraction profiles
\cite{planert2010} would provide direct marine control.

In view of these results, we deliver the first continuous, public-data,
satellite-gravity Moho for the whole Indonesian region. Calibrated against 105
seismic depths and cross-checked against AusMoho, CRUST1.0 and GEMMA, it reproduces
the first-order crustal-thickness variations, fits the seismic data to 5.8~km, and
provides an openly available regional framework for crustal structure, seismic
hazard and volcanic studies across the archipelago.
