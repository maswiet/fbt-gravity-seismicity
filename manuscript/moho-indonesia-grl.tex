\title{A Receiver-Function-Calibrated Satellite-Gravity Estimate of the Indonesian
Moho: Regional Structure and a Predictive-Skill Benchmark}

\authors{Muhammad Zuhdi\affil{1}, Sudarmaji\affil{1}, Ade Anggraini\affil{2},
Herlan Darmawan\affil{2}, Theodorus Permana\affil{2}, and
Wiwit Suryanto\affil{1,2}}
\affiliation{1}{Geophysics Laboratory, Department of Physics, Universitas Gadjah
Mada, Sekip Utara PO BOX BLS 21, Yogyakarta 55281, Indonesia}
\affiliation{2}{Seismology Research Group, Department of Physics, Universitas Gadjah
Mada, Yogyakarta 55281, Indonesia}
\correspondingauthor{Wiwit Suryanto}{ws@ugm.ac.id}

\begin{keypoints}
\item A continuous, open, receiver-function-calibrated satellite-gravity estimate of
Moho depth for the whole Indonesian archipelago
\item Benchmarked honestly: at the seismic stations it reproduces the regional mean
but does not beat trivial baselines (Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency $<0$; CRUST1.0 fits
better)
\item Its near-zero station bias is level calibration, not skill; the novel offshore
depths are unvalidated extrapolations beyond the calibration range
\end{keypoints}

\begin{abstract}
Moho depth across the Indonesian archipelago is known only from sparse, land-based
seismic stations. We estimate it from satellite gravity by adapting the fast
spherical Bott--Tikhonov tesseroid inversion of Uieda and Barbosa (2017),
calibrating two hyperparameters --- a reference depth and a single crust--mantle
density contrast ($\Delta\rho=400$~kg\,m$^{-3}$) --- against 105 receiver-function
(RF) Moho depths. The recovered surface reproduces the first-order regional pattern
(thick crust beneath the arcs and collision belts, thin crust beneath the basins)
and agrees with {CRUST1.0} and {AusMoho} at the regional scale. We then benchmark
its predictive skill transparently. At the 105 stations the estimate has a mean bias
of only $+0.2$~km --- a consequence of level calibration --- but an RMS of $6.2$~km
that does \emph{not} exceed simple baselines: a constant-mean predictor ($5.4$~km),
a three-zone regional mean ($4.6$~km) or {CRUST1.0} ($5.4$~km, with higher
correlation, $r=0.36$ vs $0.28$). The Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency is $-0.32$. The
near-perfect gravity fit ($1.2$~mGal) therefore reflects the absorption of all
anomalous mass --- sediment and subducting slab included --- into a single
interface, not resolved Moho structure. We release the estimate as a continuous,
open regional framework while cautioning explicitly that its offshore values --- its
most novel content --- are unvalidated extrapolations beyond the 20--40~km
calibration range, and that the uniform $\Delta\rho$, the $\sim$67-km gravity
bandwidth and the absence of a formal uncertainty map confine its use to first-order
regional description rather than station-scale prediction.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Plain Language Summary}
The Moho is the boundary between Earth's rigid crust and the mantle beneath it; how
deep it lies tells us where the crust is thick or thin, a basic ingredient for
studying earthquakes and volcanoes. Across Indonesia, one of the most tectonically
active regions on Earth, this depth is measured only near scattered seismic
stations, so most of the archipelago and its seas remain blank on the map. The pull
of gravity, measured uniformly from satellites, offers a way to fill these gaps:
thicker or lighter crust weighs differently, and from that signal the depth of the
Moho can be reconstructed everywhere at once. We do so for the whole of Indonesia
and then test the result honestly against 105 seismic measurements. The map captures
the broad regional pattern, but at those measurement points it is no more accurate
than simply guessing the average depth everywhere, and the widely used model CRUST1.0
is slightly better. Its new information lies offshore, where there are no seismic
measurements to check it. We therefore release it as an open, continuous regional
estimate --- useful as a starting framework and as an honest benchmark of what
satellite gravity can and cannot reveal about Indonesia's crust --- rather than as a
finished, validated map.

\section{Introduction}
The Mohorovi\v{c}i\'{c} discontinuity (Moho) marks the base of the crust, where
the P-wave velocity increases from $\sim$6.5--7 to $\sim$8~km\,s$^{-1}$ and the
density from $\sim$2.8--2.9 to $\sim$3.3~Mg\,m$^{-3}$. Its depth --- the crustal
thickness --- is a first-order constraint on the isostatic and flexural state of
the lithosphere and on the tectonic processes that shaped it \cite{watts2001}. It
is also of practical necessity: the crustal velocity model that hinges on Moho
depth underlies earthquake location, magnitude estimation and seismic-hazard
assessment. A continuous, accurate Moho is thus a foundational dataset --- and
across Indonesia it is largely missing.

Indonesia is among the most tectonically complex regions on Earth, at the junction
of the Indo-Australian, Sunda (Eurasian), Philippine Sea and Pacific plates
\cite{hamilton1979,hall2002}. Oceanic Indo-Australian lithosphere subducts along
the Sunda trench beneath Sumatra and Java; farther east the Australian continental
margin collides with the Banda arc, whose sharply curved geometry reflects slab
rollback \cite{spakman2010}; Sulawesi and Papua record collision and ophiolite
emplacement; and back-arc and marginal basins --- the Banda, Celebes and Java seas
and the Makassar Strait --- floor much of the region. Across these settings the
Moho ranges from $\sim$10~km beneath the oceanic basins to more than 40~km beneath
the arcs and collision belts, often within a few tens of kilometres. Seismic
methods --- receiver-function $Ps$ conversions and $H$--$\kappa$ stacking
\cite{zhu2000}, active-source refraction and ambient-noise tomography --- give the
most direct depths, but they sense the crust only near stations, which in Indonesia
cluster on land and along the volcanic arc \cite{zhang2023}. As a result, eastern
Indonesia and essentially all of the intervening seas remain uncharted.

Where local data are absent, two global models are commonly adopted. CRUST1.0
\cite{laske2013} is a $1^{\circ}$ compilation that, over Indonesia, is interpolated
from sparse controls; GEMMA \cite{reguzzoni2013,reguzzoni2015,rossi2022} is a
$0.5^{\circ}$ Moho inverted globally from GOCE gravity gradients under a simplified
two-layer density parameterisation. Neither is tuned to Indonesian data, and both
are too coarse to resolve the sharp arc and fore-arc contrasts of the archipelago
--- indeed, we find GEMMA to be systematically $\sim$7~km too shallow at Indonesian
seismic stations (Section~4). A dedicated, regionally calibrated model is therefore
needed.

Gravity supplies what seismology lacks here: a continuous, homogeneous field,
sensitive to the crust--mantle density contrast and available uniformly onshore and
offshore from satellite missions such as GOCE and GRACE \cite{kvas2021}. Recovering
an interface such as the Moho from gravity is, however, nonlinear and non-unique ---
a given anomaly can be reproduced by trading density contrast against depth --- so
the inversion must be regularised and anchored to independent data. \citeA{uieda2017}
solved the forward and inverse problem efficiently in \emph{spherical} coordinates,
combining Bott's \cite{bott1960} iteration, tesseroid (spherical-prism) forward
modelling \cite{uieda2016} and first-order Tikhonov regularisation, and applied it
to the South American Moho. In this work, we transfer that method, unchanged in
principle, to the whole of Indonesia; we calibrate its three hyperparameters against
an independent compilation of 105 receiver-function Moho depths, and we validate the
result against the seismic AusMoho model \cite{kennett2011} along the Australian
margin and against CRUST1.0 and GEMMA at the seismic stations. The outcome is the
first regionally calibrated, continuous, public-data gravity Moho for the archipelago (global models such as GEMMA already provide a coarse, uncalibrated one).

\section{Data and Methods}
We use the satellite-only gravity model GOCO06s \cite{kvas2021}, synthesised to
spherical-harmonic degree/order 300 on a regular grid at 4~km constant height, and,
as a higher-resolution variant, the altimetry free-air anomaly \cite{sandwell2014};
Earth relief is from SRTM15+ \cite{tozer2019}. Validation rests on our compilation of 105
receiver-function (RF) Moho depths across Indonesia \cite{fauzi2021}
($96.3^{\circ}$--$140.7^{\circ}$E, $10.2^{\circ}$S--$5.2^{\circ}$N; 20--40~km deep,
mean 30.4~km; 31 on Sumatra, 24 on Java, 50 in eastern Indonesia, none offshore;
Table~S1, Figure~\ref{fig:compare}a), obtained by $H$--$\kappa$ stacking of teleseismic
$Ps$ conversions. Being derived from seismic velocities, these depths are independent
of the gravity field inverted here. The most comprehensive comparable compilation is
\citeA{zhang2023}.

The gravity disturbance is the observed field minus the closed-form WGS84 normal
gravity,
\begin{equation}
\delta(P) = g(P) - \gamma(P),
\label{eq:disturbance}
\end{equation}
so that it retains only the effect of masses anomalous with respect to the
reference ellipsoid. Every mass effect is forward modelled with tesseroids
(spherical prisms) that honour Earth curvature \cite{uieda2016}: a tesseroid of
density $\rho$ produces at the computation point $P=(r_P,\phi_P,\lambda_P)$ the
radial attraction
\begin{equation}
g_z(P) = G\rho \int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}\!\!\int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2}\!\!
\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{(r_P-r\cos\psi)\,r^{2}\cos\phi}{\ell^{3}}
\;dr\,d\phi\,d\lambda,
\label{eq:tesseroid}
\end{equation}
with $\ell=(r_P^{2}+r^{2}-2r_Pr\cos\psi)^{1/2}$ and
$\cos\psi=\sin\phi_P\sin\phi+\cos\phi_P\cos\phi\cos(\lambda-\lambda_P)$, evaluated
by Gauss--Legendre quadrature \cite{uieda2016}. Removing the topographic and
bathymetric effect (land density $2670$; ocean contrast $-1640$~kg\,m$^{-3}$, i.e.\ sea water $1030$ replacing rock $2670$)
leaves the Bouguer disturbance, our data vector $\mathbf{d}^{o}$. The anomalous Moho
is one tesseroid per grid cell between the reference depth $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and
the true depth $z_k$ (the unknowns $\mathbf{p}$), with contrast $-\Delta\rho$ where
the Moho is deeper than $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $+\Delta\rho$ where shallower
\cite{uieda2017}.

We estimate $\mathbf{p}$ by minimising the regularised goal function
\begin{equation}
\Gamma(\mathbf{p}) = \big\|\mathbf{d}^{o}-\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{p})\big\|^{2}
 + \mu\,\big\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{p}\big\|^{2},
\label{eq:goal}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}$ is the first-difference operator and $\mu$ the regularisation
weight. Following \citeA{bott1960} and \citeA{silva2014}, the dense Jacobian is
replaced by the diagonal Bouguer-plate form
$\mathbf{A}=-2\pi G\,\Delta\rho\,\mathbf{I}$ (the minus sign expressing that a
deeper Moho lowers gravity), so each Gauss--Newton step solves a sparse, constant,
symmetric-positive-definite system. Depths are clipped to 3--70~km and iterated to
convergence; for $\mu=0$ this reduces to the classical Bott update,
\begin{equation}
\Delta z_j = \frac{d^{o}_j - d_j(\mathbf{p})}{2\pi G\,\Delta\rho}.
\label{eq:bott}
\end{equation}
The three hyperparameters are set in two steps \cite{uieda2017}: $\mu$ by hold-out
cross-validation on the gravity data (30\% of nodes withheld), then
$(z_{\mathrm{ref}},\Delta\rho)$ against the seismic Moho (Figure~S2). Because only
these two scalars are tuned against the 105 depths, we guard against over-fitting
with a spatially blocked five-fold cross-validation: stations are grouped by
$k$-means on location, $(z_{\mathrm{ref}},\Delta\rho)$ are re-selected on each
fold's training stations, and the held-out stations are scored.

\textbf{Absolute level and the tie to AusMoho.} The inversion determines the Moho
\emph{relief} from the Bouguer disturbance; its \emph{absolute} level is carried by
the two global scalars $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\Delta\rho$. Through the reference
model and the plate relation~(\ref{eq:bott}), a deeper $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$ or a
smaller $\Delta\rho$ shifts and rescales the entire surface without changing its
shape, so fixing this pair against seismic depths is what pins the model to an
absolute datum. The 105 RF depths \cite{fauzi2021} set that level; AusMoho \cite{kennett2011} is
\emph{not} an input to the inversion, does not enter the objective function, and
therefore cannot ``anchor'' the model --- it can only serve as an
\emph{a-posteriori} consistency comparison, which we report as such in Section~4.
Full derivations, diagnostics and a sediment test are in Text~S1 and
Figures~S1--S2.

\section{Results}
The regularisation weight $\mu$ sets how much smoothing the inversion imposes: a
larger $\mu$ forces a smoother Moho at the cost of fitting the gravity data less
closely. Hold-out cross-validation on the gravity data returns a very small weight
($\mu=10^{-10}$). We do not over-interpret this: with strongly autocorrelated nodes
a random hold-out mainly measures how predictable a node is from its neighbours, so
near-zero $\mu$ reflects a smooth, easily interpolated gravity field, not proof that
the Moho is uniquely resolved without smoothing. The seismic misfit, in
turn, decreases monotonically with $\Delta\rho$ --- a
density--depth trade-off whose raw minimum runs to unphysical contrasts --- but the
fit is only weakly sensitive over the physical range: at $z_{\mathrm{ref}}=35$~km
the RMS is 6.2, 6.0, 5.9 and 5.8~km at $\Delta\rho=400,450,500,600$~kg\,m$^{-3}$. We
therefore adopt the physical $\Delta\rho=400$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ --- the value used by
\citet{uieda2017} and within the 280--450~kg\,m$^{-3}$ range of \citet{christensen1995}
--- even though the seismic RMS keeps decreasing to higher, unphysical contrasts
(the density--depth trade-off); the fit changes by only $<0.5$~km across
400--600~kg\,m$^{-3}$, and a single uniform contrast over oceanic-to-continental
crust remains a simplification.
The reference depth, by contrast, is well resolved: at $\Delta\rho=400$~kg\,m$^{-3}$
the seismic RMS has a sharp minimum (7.7, 6.5, 6.2, 6.9~km at
$z_{\mathrm{ref}}=30, 32.5, 35, 37.5$~km), so the absolute level is constrained to
$\pm2$--$3$~km; we adopt $z_{\mathrm{ref}}=35$~km, independently supported by AusMoho
(below). Bott's iteration converged in 15--20 steps (RMS-misfit change below
0.15~km).

The preferred model (Figure~\ref{fig:moho}) yields Moho depths of $\sim$11--59~km:
thick (35--45~km) beneath the Sunda, Sulawesi and Banda arcs and the Papuan
highlands, and thin (10--20~km) beneath the marginal and oceanic basins, with the
35-km contour outlining the arc and continental blocks. It differs from the 105
seismic depths by $+0.2\pm6.2$~km (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}), as good as the
identical method in South America (1.2, 6.8~km); the median is $+0.8$~km, the
interquartile range 8.3~km, and 62\% and 88\% of stations fall within $\pm5$ and
$\pm10$~km.

\textbf{Predictive-skill benchmark.} We test the estimate against trivial and
recalibrated baselines at the 105 stations (Table~\ref{tab:skill}). Its near-zero
bias ($+0.2$~km) is a direct consequence of calibrating the level against these
depths, not a measure of skill. Its RMS ($6.2$~km) is \emph{larger} than a
constant-mean predictor ($5.4$~km), a three-zone regional mean ($4.6$~km) and
CRUST1.0 ($5.4$~km), and CRUST1.0 correlates better ($r=0.36$ vs $0.28$). The
Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency relative to the constant mean is $-0.32$: at the seismic
control points the gravity estimate does not improve on predicting the average depth
everywhere. A five-fold spatially blocked cross-validation confirms the two
calibrated scalars are stable (every fold selects the same pair), but this tests
their stability, not the spatial fidelity of the map; and because gravity nodes are
strongly autocorrelated on the $0.5^{\circ}$ grid, the random-node selection of
$\mu\to0$ is prone to spatial leakage and should not be read as evidence that the
data pin the Moho without smoothing.

The recovered surface nonetheless reproduces the gravity field almost exactly: the
residual after inversion is only $1.2$~mGal (Figure~\ref{fig:gravres}). That
near-perfect fit is the concern, not the reassurance --- with a single interface and
$\mu\to0$, all anomalous mass (sediment, arc and slab density, lateral
heterogeneity) is mapped into Moho relief rather than resolved as Moho. The low
station skill then follows from an amplitude compression: an ordinary-least-squares
fit of estimated on seismic depth has a slope of only $0.26$
(Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}), so the thinnest crust is overestimated and the thickest
underestimated. CRUST1.0 shows a similar slope ($0.23$), and both are limited by the
$\sim$67-km gravity bandwidth --- an even coarser \emph{effective} Moho resolution
after downward continuation; neither resolves the sharp arc/fore-arc contrasts.
Refining the grid to $0.25^{\circ}$ adds no information (Table~S2).

\textbf{Consistency with AusMoho.} As a regional consistency comparison (not a
constraint), we compare the model with the seismic AusMoho compilation
\cite{kennett2011} along the Australian margin. The comparison is only partly
independent: our southern and western Indonesian RF depths \cite{fauzi2021} were
themselves contributed to the updated AusMoho surface (B.~L.~N.~Kennett, personal
communication, 2022), so at the shared margin the two are consistent by
construction. Farther south, over the northern Australian mainland
($15^{\circ}$--$11.5^{\circ}$S), the model and AusMoho agree with a mean difference
of $-1.9$~km and $r=0.87$. This is encouraging for the \emph{absolute level}, but
the correlation is over two smooth surfaces sharing a strong regional gradient: with
a spatial correlation length of $\sim$100--200~km the $N=369$ grid cells represent
only of order ten independent samples, so $r=0.87$ should not be read as a
formal skill estimate. A detrended, effective-sample-size analysis is left for
future work. Crust thickens to 39--50~km at the Timor--Banda collision front.

\textbf{Global models.} At the 105 stations (Table~\ref{tab:skill},
Table~\ref{tab:compare}) CRUST1.0 is the more accurate reference (RMS 5.4 vs
6.2~km; $r=0.36$ vs 0.28); our estimate's only edge is a smaller bias, which comes
from calibration. Raw GEMMA is far too shallow (bias $-7.4$~km, RMS 10.2~km), but
this comparison is not equal-footing: our model was levelled against the RF depths
and GEMMA was not. When GEMMA is given the same freedom --- an affine recalibration
$a+b\,\mathrm{GEMMA}$ fitted to these stations --- its RMS falls to $5.4$~km, i.e.\
it matches the constant-mean baseline and \emph{beats} our estimate. This tempers
the ``global models are inadequate'' claim: raw GEMMA carries a regional bias, but
a simple offset/affine correction of GEMMA is as good as, or better than, the full
inversion at the seismic points. Spatially (Figure~\ref{fig:compare}), our model
agrees with CRUST1.0 to within $\pm5$~km in the continental interior and is deeper
along the Sunda--Java trench (where the diagonal Jacobian folds slab mass into the
Moho); grid-to-grid it reproduces both CRUST1.0's and GEMMA's patterns (slopes 0.97
and 1.15). The genuine, if modest, contribution is therefore continuous coverage and
a physically levelled surface, not superior station-scale accuracy.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
From a satellite field limited to $\sim$67-km wavelengths, and under a single-density
inversion, we obtain a continuous surface that reproduces the first-order crustal
architecture of Indonesia: thick crust
follows the volcanic arc and the Sulawesi--Banda--Papua collision belts, thin crust
marks the oceanic marginal basins, and the Holocene volcanoes sit along the
thick-crust arc (Figures~S3--S4). This first-order picture is not itself new --- it
is expected from topography, regional tectonics, CRUST1.0 and the RF compilation ---
and, as the benchmark above shows, the estimate does not add station-scale skill
over CRUST1.0 or a regional mean. Its contribution is a single, continuous,
physically levelled surface across land and sea, not a sharper image of crustal
structure.

Against the wider RF literature the picture is consistent but not a validation.
Sampled at 18 RF depths from five other studies
\cite{kieling2011,macpherson2012,bora2016,syuhada2016,anggono2020} not used in
calibration, the model has a small overall bias ($-0.8$~km) and regional ranges that
match those reported for Sumatra, Central Java (27--39~km) and West Java (25--37~km)
and the pan-Indonesian range of \citet{zhang2023} (24--38~km). But the station
scatter is large (RMS 9.0~km) and the studies disagree strongly among themselves
(biases of $+11.4$~km for the fore-arc-focused \citealt{macpherson2012} and
$-10.0$~km for \citealt{syuhada2016}; Figure~S5), so this ``ensemble'' only bounds
the absolute level --- it cannot confirm the map's spatial detail. The persistent
$\sim$5~km underestimate at the deepest-crust southern-Java stations reflects the
amplitude compression above.

The limitations follow directly from the method and data. Resolution is set by the
satellite gravity, not the grid, and a single, uniform $\Delta\rho$ over
oceanic-to-continental crust is a simplification. Because the diagonal Jacobian
assigns all anomalous gravity to the Moho, the subducting Sunda--Banda slabs are not
separated and can deepen the estimate near the trenches; we therefore flag Moho
values within $\sim$50~km of the Sunda--Banda subduction front as slab-contaminated
and less reliable, consistent with the deeper-than-CRUST1.0 signature and the largest
residuals there. Most importantly, the validation is land-biased: the offshore and
oceanic-basin Moho (11--15~km), where the model is most novel, rests on no seismic
control and should be read as prediction; wide-angle refraction profiles at the Sunda
margin \citep{planert2010} are the natural data for direct marine validation in
future work. We provide no formal uncertainty map; the single $6.2$~km station RMS
cannot be applied uniformly and would be far larger offshore, near the domain edges,
and away from the RF control. Building a proper ensemble uncertainty (over gravity
errors, topographic density, spatially variable $\Delta\rho$, sediment and slab
corrections, and $z_{\mathrm{ref}}$) is the priority for turning this estimate into a
usable reference model.

We therefore present a continuous, openly available, receiver-function-calibrated
satellite-gravity \emph{estimate} of the Indonesian Moho, together with a transparent
benchmark of what it can and cannot do. It reproduces the first-order regional
crustal-thickness pattern and a physically reasonable absolute level, but at the
seismic control points it does not outperform a constant-mean or regional-mean
predictor, or CRUST1.0; its most novel content, offshore, is an unvalidated
extrapolation; and a simple affine correction of GEMMA is comparably accurate at the
stations. Its value is as an open, physically consistent regional framework and as a
cautionary benchmark of the resolution and skill that satellite-gravity Moho
inversion can realistically deliver over Indonesia --- not as a validated,
station-scale predictive map.
